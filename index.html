Nothing here has forgotten. It has only been waiting for someone who could read it.

Sculpture, drawing, soundscape and simulation — built around what still grows in places nobody comes to anymore.

What Grows There

Eastern Germany, Czechia, Latvia, Poland. 2023—

Mourners brought plants to these graves for generations. Species used in Ashkenazi herbalism were carried in, planted, left behind — and many of them naturalized. The people stopped coming. The plants did not stop growing.

So the flora of a Jewish cemetery in Central or Eastern Europe is not incidental to it. It is a record, held in living tissue, of a practice and a population that were ended. The environment carries the memory of who used to persist there, and it keeps carrying it under neglect — sometimes more faithfully than under maintenance.

The work runs in three registers: botanical fieldwork, a laser survey, and a simulation. The argument is the same in each. These sites hold ecological value and memorial value at once, and managing them well means refusing to trade either away.

Reading the flora

Ethnobotanical field survey across four countries

Cataloguing which herbalism species are still present, where they sit relative to the stones, and how their distribution shifts across sites under very different regimes of care — from actively groomed grounds to ones under fifty years of forest.

Alongside the species lists, qualitative records of the flora and fauna that have moved in.

The scan

LiDAR survey with the Department of Culture, Rzeszów

A three-dimensional archive of the cemetery's exact topography at the moment the scans were taken — every leaning stone, every depression in the ground, every canopy edge, fixed as a point cloud.

It is a strange kind of preservation. It saves nothing physical and stops no decay. What it holds is a single instant of a place that is still changing, which turns out to be the same thing a memorial does.

Broken Eden

Simulation built in Unreal Engine 5, projected onto tulle and lace

We lived contentedly out of time in the kalyna hedge, until bombs cratered either side. There were two terrible winters when the waxwings would not come, even for the latest berries in the leanest freezes. The family had no choice but to relocate.

The cemetery as a city of the dead, where green and gray diffuse in distant memory. A space to move through at your own pace, without a guide — the scan data made walkable, so that the plants are found rather than pointed at.

The projection falls through layered tulle and lace and never resolves on a single surface. The image sits in the air at several depths at once, present and not quite legible, which is the condition the work is about.

Watchers: The End

Wood, mold-made and taxidermied elements, sound. dimensions year

A double tower reaching upward and protruding down through itself. At the top, a feathered bird-woman staring into the stars on the final night.

The piece required skills she did not have when she started it. Over a year she learned the carpentry needed to build the tower, and mold-making and taxidermy to make the bird-woman. It was shown with live soundscapes mixed to accompany the end of a world adjacent to our own, and later disassembled, transported, and rebuilt in the CCRMA Listening Room at Stanford.

Breezemonger

Album. Trust the Crow with Artyom. Released 18 July year

this album is cold and rough. noisy and diffuse like the wind. grating and sincere like a smile in the mirror. Breezemonger sees no difference between a piano recorded from the other side of the room, a plucky synth right in your ear, and a hoarse voice turning in circles beneath the ground — they should all be heard at once. it took a while to make, and, in collaboration with Trust the Crow and Artyom, we have created something truly itself.

One Molecule Said Yes to Another (And Life Was Born)

120710 Gallery, 1207 Tenth St., Berkeley. Curated by Stephanie Zhang

A group exhibition of fifteen artists in a repurposed industrial metal building, working across living fungi sculptures, mammoth technology, cuttlefish-inspired camouflaging materials, and haptic gardens. About

Irina Spector Delgass works in sculpture, wood and welded metal, soundscape and music, painting and drawing, and in simulation and worldbuilding that gather the rest of it together. She records and performs as Trust the Crow. Birds turn up in nearly all of it. So does geographic melancholy and persistence — landscapes that have been left, and what carries on in them regardless.

She double majored in Slavic Studies and Geophysics, writing an honors thesis in each, and the two halves have converged rather than competed. The cemetery work grew out of ethnobotanical fieldwork in Eastern Germany, Czechia, Latvia, and Poland, and is now equally a body of artwork: a plant survey, a LiDAR archive made with the Department of Culture in Rzeszów, and Broken Eden, a simulation built from the scan data. The Slavic Studies thesis argues that historical and memorial sites should be managed for their ecological value alongside their memorial function.

On the geophysics side, a thesis on wave attenuation over kelp-planted artificial reefs. She is specializing further in ecology and organismal biology and carrying the physics across: how organisms move through fluids, how fluid dynamics shapes ecology, collective behavior, flying and swimming locomotion, and the interaction between the built environment and the living one. It is the same interest that puts birds in the sculpture.

Between terms she took a course in plant identification and orienteering in rural Vermont, navigating unfamiliar forest by map and compass, day and night, learning species by common and Latin name.
